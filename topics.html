Topic Index of Garrett's Library

This page indexes every book in Garrett's library by topic. It covers 56 topics and 148 books. Each entry gives the title, the author and what the book actually holds or argues on that topic. Topics are listed A–Z, and a per-book index follows them. Only books in the library are listed; claims are kept modest, and uncertain attributions are marked.

Aesthetics and Art

  • A Philosophy of Music Education (Second Edition), Bennett Reimer: Reimer argues music education should be 'aesthetic education', developing students' feelingful responsiveness to the expressive qualities of music; the second edition broadens this toward synergy with praxial views.
  • Doktor Faustus, Thomas Mann: Mann's novel makes the composer Leverkühn's demonic pact a figure for art that breaks through sterility at the cost of humanity, entangling modern music with German catastrophe.
  • On the Aesthetic Education of Man, Friedrich Schiller: Schiller argues that beauty, through the 'play drive' that reconciles sense and form, is the path by which humanity becomes morally and politically free.
  • Philosophy of Poetry (The Genius of Lucretius), Henri Bergson: Bergson argues that Lucretius's genius lies in making philosophy felt poetically, poetry being a vehicle of intuition rather than decoration.
  • Selected Essays of William Hazlitt, William Hazlitt: Hazlitt defends 'gusto', the power of passion in art, and judges painting and literature by their vividness of feeling rather than rules.
  • The Birth of Tragedy and The Case of Wagner, Friedrich Nietzsche: Nietzsche argues that Greek tragedy fused the Apollonian (form, image) and Dionysian (intoxication, unity) and that 'only as an aesthetic phenomenon is existence justified'; the Case of Wagner turns this into a polemic against Wagner as decadent.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato holds that art is imitation of appearances and morally dangerous, the founding statement of the 'ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry'.
  • The Romantic Rebellion, Kenneth Clark: Clark presents Romantic painting (roughly 1790–1860) as a rebellion of passion, imagination and the sublime against classical order, through figures such as Goya, Blake, Turner, Delacroix and Géricault.
  • The World as Will and Representation, Vol. I, Arthur Schopenhauer: Schopenhauer holds that aesthetic contemplation briefly frees us from the will by grasping the Platonic Ideas, and that music copies the will itself rather than any idea.

American Culture and Society

  • Babbitt, Sinclair Lewis: Lewis satirizes the conformity, boosterism and spiritual emptiness of the Midwestern businessman, whose brief rebellion ends in surrender.
  • Infinite Jest, David Foster Wallace: Wallace's novel portrays an America of addiction and entertainment so compelling that it destroys the capacity for attention and sincere feeling.
  • Light in August, William Faulkner: Faulkner shows Southern racial categories as murderous fictions through Joe Christmas, who does not know whether he is Black or white.
  • The American Lyceum, Author uncertain: A study of the nineteenth-century lyceum lecture movement as democratic adult education (edition and author not confirmed).
  • The Culture of Narcissism: American Life in an Age of Diminishing Expectations, Christopher Lasch: Lasch argues that late-twentieth-century America cultivates a narcissistic personality (dependent, anxious, hungry for admiration) produced by bureaucracy, therapy culture, consumerism and the decline of authority and the family.

American History and Founding

  • Daniel Boone, and the Hunters of Kentucky, W. H. Bogart (1854): A popular 1854 narrative that treats Boone and the Kentucky hunters as heroic founders of the frontier.
  • George Washington: The Making of an American Symbol, Barry Schwartz: Schwartz, a sociologist, argues that Washington's heroic image was constructed by eighteenth-century Americans to embody republican virtue, making him a symbol of national unity.
  • John Adams, Vol. I (1735–1784), Page Smith: Page Smith's Pulitzer biography, drawing on the Adams Papers, portrays Adams's path from Braintree lawyer through the Revolution and diplomacy as a man of fierce integrity and vanity.
  • John Adams, Vol. II (1784–1826), Page Smith: Volume II follows Adams's vice presidency, presidency (including the XYZ affair and Alien and Sedition Acts) and long retirement, presenting him sympathetically as an independent statesman.
  • Lafayette Letters, Edited by Edward Everett Dale: Lafayette's letters show the transatlantic link between the American and French revolutions from the perspective of a liberal aristocrat devoted to Washington.
  • Life of Lincoln, Farm and Fireside Library, 1896: A popular 1896 life of Lincoln, more moral and patriotic tribute than critical history.
  • Mason & Dixon, Thomas Pynchon: Pynchon's novel uses the surveyors who drew the Mason–Dixon line to meditate on how the line-drawing of Enlightenment reason and slavery shaped America before its founding.
  • Political Essays, James Russell Lowell: Lowell writes as an antislavery Unionist, attacking slavery and the secessionist South and defending the Union cause during and after the Civil War.
  • The Era of Franklin D. Roosevelt, Denis W. Brogan: Brogan, a British observer, interprets the New Deal and the war years as a pragmatic American response to crisis rather than a revolution.

Ancient Greece and Rome

  • A History of the Ancient World, second edition, Chester G. Starr: Starr's survey covers the ancient Near East, Greece and Rome as one connected Mediterranean story, stressing social and economic as well as political history.
  • Alexander: The Ends of the Earth, Valerio Massimo Manfredi: Manfredi's historical novel (final volume of his Alexander trilogy) dramatizes Alexander's campaigns to India and his death.
  • Euripides III, Euripides (ed. David Grene and Richmond Lattimore): Euripides' plays show gods and men in morally unsettling conflict, with sympathy for women, outsiders and the victims of war.
  • Greek Science in Antiquity, Marshall Clagett: Clagett traces Greek science from the Presocratics through Hellenistic mathematics, astronomy and medicine, and follows its transmission into late antiquity.
  • Lives of the Caesars, Suetonius: Suetonius's biographies of Julius Caesar through Domitian arrange private habits, vices and omens by topic, giving a scandal-rich but invaluable portrait of imperial power.
  • The Complete Poems of Cavafy, C. P. Cavafy (tr. Rae Dalven): Cavafy's poems revisit Hellenistic and Roman-era Alexandria and Antioch, treating history ironically and with sympathy for losers and the declining.
  • The Iliad, Homer: Homer's epic of Achilles' wrath at Troy shows honor, mortality and grief as the core of the heroic ethos.
  • The Life of Greece, Will Durant: Durant presents Greek civilization to the Roman conquest as the fountainhead of Western philosophy, art and democracy, in a narrative, humanistic style.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Vico draws heavily on Roman law and early Roman history, and on Homer, to reconstruct the heroic age of the Greeks and Romans.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Set in the Piraeus, the dialogue reflects Athenian debates about democracy, Sparta and the sophists.
  • The Twelve Caesars, Michael Grant: Grant retells the first twelve emperors using Suetonius critically, weighing the gossip against other evidence.
  • Thirty-Eight Latin Stories, Anne H. Groton and James M. May: Graded Latin readings adapted from Roman myth and history for students.
  • Wheelock’s Latin, Frederic M. Wheelock: The standard introductory Latin grammar, with readings drawn from classical authors.

Authority, Tradition and Counter-Revolution

  • Libido Dominandi: Sexual Liberation and Political Control, E. Michael Jones: Jones, a Catholic traditionalist, argues that sexual liberation has served since the Enlightenment as a means of political and social control.
  • Major Works, Vol. I, Joseph de Maistre: Maistre argues that political order rests on providence, sacrifice and authority rather than contracts, and that the French Revolution was a divine punishment.
  • Revolt Against the Modern World, Julius Evola: Evola contrasts a sacred, hierarchical 'world of Tradition' with modernity, which he sees as an inversion and decline from that order.
  • Ride the Tiger, Julius Evola: Evola advises the 'differentiated man' to 'ride the tiger' of modern nihilism, staying inwardly detached instead of fighting or joining a dissolving world.
  • The Decline of the West, Vol. II, Oswald Spengler: Spengler holds that each culture hardens into a 'civilization' of cities, money and Caesarism; the West has entered this late phase.

Being and Ontology

  • Being and Time, Martin Heidegger: Heidegger reopens the 'question of the meaning of Being' by analyzing Dasein, the being for whom its own being is an issue, and argues that Being must be understood through temporality.
  • Difference and Repetition, Gilles Deleuze: Deleuze argues that being is univocal and that difference in itself, not identity, is primary; identity is a secondary effect of repetition and difference.
  • Ethics, Benedict de Spinoza: Spinoza deduces in geometric order that there is only one substance with infinite attributes, of which thought and extension are the two known to us.
  • Logic, Martin Heidegger: In this lecture course Heidegger argues that logic must be grounded in a questioning of language and the human being rather than treated as formal calculation.
  • Process and Reality, corrected edition, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead replaces enduring substances with 'actual occasions' of experience that become and perish, making process the basic reality.
  • The Metaphysics, Aristotle (tr. Hugh Lawson-Tancred, Penguin Classics): Aristotle defines first philosophy as the study of being qua being and argues that substance (ousia), above all form, is the primary sense of being, culminating in the unmoved mover.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato holds that the Forms are fully real, while sensible things are only images of them, as the shadows in the cave are images of objects.
  • Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Ludwig Wittgenstein: Wittgenstein holds that the world is the totality of facts, not things, and that logical form is shared by world and language.

Biography and Character

  • Autobiography of Bertrand Russell, Bertrand Russell: Russell recounts his life in logic, pacifism, love and politics, centring it on three passions: longing for love, the search for knowledge and pity for suffering.
  • Carlyle, Emery Neff: Neff's 1932 critical biography follows Carlyle from Scottish Calvinism through his German Romantic influences to his prophetic social criticism.
  • Frederick the Great (Vols. I–IV), Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle presents Frederick II as the last genuine king, a hero of work and reality amid an age of sham, tracing Prussia's rise.
  • Impressions of Lenin, Angelica Balabanoff: Balabanoff, who worked closely with Lenin in the Zimmerwald movement and early Comintern, gives a personal portrait of his ascetic will and his ruthless, ends-justify-means politics.
  • James Joyce, Richard Ellmann: Ellmann's biography shows how closely Joyce's fiction was built from the people and events of his own life.
  • John Milton: Englishman, James Holly Hanford: Hanford argues that Milton was deeply English, his poetry bound up with English politics, Puritanism and the Civil War.
  • Living Biographies of Famous Rulers, Henry Thomas and Dana Lee Thomas: Popular short lives of famous monarchs and rulers, written for general readers.
  • Oscar Wilde, Hesketh Pearson: Pearson's biography portrays Wilde sympathetically as a brilliant talker and wit destroyed by his trials.
  • Reminiscences, Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle's memoirs of his father, Jane Welsh Carlyle, Edward Irving and others mix tender grief with remorse, especially for his wife.
  • Representative Man: Ralph Waldo Emerson in His Time, Joel Porte: Porte reads Emerson in the context of his own time and how he shaped himself as the 'representative man' of American culture.
  • Stalin, Leon Trotsky: Trotsky's unfinished biography portrays Stalin as a mediocrity raised by the bureaucracy, and is also a self-justifying indictment.
  • Warren French’s biography of John Steinbeck, Warren French: French surveys Steinbeck's life and works, relating his fiction to his biological, 'non-teleological' view of human groups.

Carlyle

  • Carlyle, Emery Neff: Neff traces Carlyle's intellectual development from Scottish Calvinism through German idealism to social prophecy.
  • Frederick the Great (Vols. I–IV), Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle's history of Frederick II embodies his late hero-worship and contempt for 'shams'.
  • Literary Essays (Vols. II & IV), James Russell Lowell: Lowell's essay on Carlyle (Vol. II) admires his early genius but deplores his later worship of force.
  • Reminiscences, Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle's memoirs of his father, wife and friends reveal his Calvinist upbringing and his grief and remorse.

China

  • China, Felix Greene: Greene, a sympathetic Western visitor, argues that Mao's China of the early 1960s had overcome famine and misery, and that Western reports misrepresented it.

Christianity and the Church

  • A History of Christianity, Volume I: Beginnings to 1500, Kenneth Scott Latourette: Latourette, a Baptist historian, narrates Christianity from Jesus to 1500 as an expanding movement, with attention to missions and to its repeated renewals.
  • Concluding Unscientific Postscript, Søren Kierkegaard (tr. Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong): Kierkegaard (as Climacus) argues that becoming a Christian is a matter of passionate inward appropriation, not objective proof or membership of Christendom.
  • George Herbert, Stanley Stewart: Stewart reads George Herbert's The Temple as devotional poetry shaped by the English Church's liturgy and order.
  • Holy Bible (American Standard Version, with references), American Standard Version: The ASV (1901) is a literal American revision of the King James Bible; it is the primary text of Christian scripture.
  • The Brothers Karamazov, Fyodor Dostoevsky: Through Zosima and Alyosha, Dostoevsky defends active Christian love against Ivan's rebellion and the Grand Inquisitor's offer of bread, miracle and authority.
  • The Essential Writings of Christian Mysticism, Associated with Bernard McGinn: This McGinn anthology gathers Christian mystics from early centuries through the modern period on contemplation and union with God.
  • The Name of the Rose, Umberto Eco: Eco's medieval detective novel sets Franciscan poverty disputes and the Inquisition against a hunt for Aristotle's lost book on comedy.

Civilization, Rise and Decline

  • A History of the Western World, Shepard B. Clough, Nina G. Garsoian, David L. Hicks, Peter Gay, David J. Brandenburg, et al. (Heath): A multi-author college survey of Western civilization from antiquity to the twentieth century.
  • Revolt Against the Modern World, Julius Evola: Evola reads history as a long fall from a primordial, sacred civilization to the materialist modern world.
  • The Decline of the West, Vol. II, Oswald Spengler: Spengler treats cultures as organisms with lifespans; Volume II covers cities, peoples, the state, money and religion in the decline of the West.
  • The Legacy of Israel, Various contributors: Essays by various scholars on the contribution of ancient Israel's religion, law and literature to Western civilization.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Vico traces how nations arise from religion, marriage and burial, mature into reasoned law, and decay through luxury and the 'barbarism of reflection' before beginning the cycle again.
  • The Outline of History, H. G. Wells: Wells writes a world history from the earth's origins onward, aiming at a common human story and a future world-state.
  • The Philosophy of History, G. W. F. Hegel (tr. J. Sibree): Hegel reads history moving from the Oriental world through Greece and Rome to the Germanic world, in increasing consciousness of freedom.

Communism and Marxism

  • Capitalist Realism: Is There No Alternative?, Mark Fisher: Fisher argues that 'capitalist realism' makes it seem impossible to imagine an alternative to capitalism.
  • China, Felix Greene: Greene, a sympathetic Western visitor, reports favorably on Mao's China of the early 1960s.
  • Impressions of Lenin, Angelica Balabanoff: Balabanoff, a disillusioned former colleague, describes how Lenin's devotion to revolution led him to accept any means.
  • Stalin, Leon Trotsky: Trotsky indicts Stalin as the gravedigger of the revolution.
  • The Essential Works of Lenin, V. I. Lenin: Lenin argues that imperialism is capitalism's highest stage and that a vanguard party must lead the revolution to a dictatorship of the proletariat.

Consciousness and Mind

  • Creative Evolution, Henri Bergson: Bergson holds that consciousness is coextensive with life's élan vital and that intellect and instinct are diverging tools of it.
  • Critique of Pure Reason, Immanuel Kant: Kant holds that the 'I think' (transcendental unity of apperception) must be able to accompany all representations, but that we cannot know the soul as a substance.
  • Gödel, Escher, Bach, Douglas Hofstadter: Hofstadter argues that the self and consciousness emerge from 'strange loops' of self-reference in a formal system, linking Gödel, Escher and Bach.
  • Ideas, Edmund Husserl: Husserl brackets the natural attitude to describe pure consciousness, whose essence is intentionality, the noesis–noema structure through which objects are constituted.
  • Man and His Symbols, Carl G. Jung: Jung holds that conscious life rests on a personal and collective unconscious expressed in symbols and dreams.
  • Meta-Meditations, Sesonske and Fleming: These essays use Descartes' Meditations as a starting point for the cogito, doubt and the mind–body problem.
  • Mind: A Brief Introduction, John R. Searle: Searle argues for biological naturalism: consciousness is a real, subjective feature caused by brain processes, and computer programs alone cannot produce understanding (the Chinese Room).

Critique of Modernity

  • Capitalist Realism: Is There No Alternative?, Mark Fisher: Fisher argues that neoliberal capitalism has produced widespread depression, bureaucratic absurdity and the 'slow cancellation of the future'.
  • Libido Dominandi: Sexual Liberation and Political Control, E. Michael Jones: Jones argues that modern sexual liberation, from the Marquis de Sade to the sexual revolution, served as an instrument of social control.
  • Limits to Medicine, Ivan Illich: Illich argues that professionalized institutions such as medicine disable people's own capacity to cope.
  • Revolt Against the Modern World, Julius Evola: Evola condemns modernity (individualism, democracy, materialism) as the last, dissolving phase of a cyclical decline.
  • Simulacra and Simulation, Jean Baudrillard: Baudrillard argues that media and consumer society have dissolved the distinction between reality and its representations.
  • The Culture of Narcissism: American Life in an Age of Diminishing Expectations, Christopher Lasch: Lasch argues that modern capitalism and the therapeutic state erode the family and inner life, producing dependent, anxious narcissists.
  • The Decline of the West, Vol. II, Oswald Spengler: Spengler sees the modern West as a late 'civilization' ruled by money and mass cities, headed toward Caesarism.
  • The Sacred and the Profane, Mircea Eliade: Eliade argues that modern secular man inhabits a desacralized world but still carries camouflaged religious behaviour.
  • The Technological Society, Jacques Ellul: Ellul holds that modern societies serve technique rather than human ends, and that politics and economics merely adapt to its demands.

Death and Mortality

  • Being and Time, Martin Heidegger: Heidegger argues that authentic existence is 'being-toward-death', anticipating one's own death as one's ownmost possibility.
  • Limits to Medicine, Ivan Illich: Illich argues that modern medicine has expropriated people's ability to face pain and death, turning death into a technical event.
  • The Iliad, Homer: The Iliad confronts heroic glory with death, culminating in Priam and Achilles grieving together.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato argues for the soul's immortality and ends with the Myth of Er, in which souls are judged after death and choose their next lives.
  • The Tragedy of King Lear, William Shakespeare (intro & notes by Mary Barron): Lear's suffering and Cordelia's death present a world in which justice and consolation may fail.

Education

  • A Philosophy of Music Education (Second Edition), Bennett Reimer: Reimer argues that music education's core value lies in developing aesthetic, feelingful experience.
  • On Experience, Nature, and Freedom, John Dewey: Dewey holds that education is the reconstruction of experience, with learning arising from active inquiry rather than passive reception.
  • On the Aesthetic Education of Man, Friedrich Schiller: Schiller holds that aesthetic education is the necessary preparation for moral and political freedom.
  • The American Lyceum, Author uncertain: The lyceum movement is presented as a form of public adult education in nineteenth-century America (details unconfirmed).
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato outlines the guardians' education, from censored music and gymnastics to mathematics and dialectic, and defines education as turning the soul toward the light rather than filling it with knowledge.

Enlightenment

  • Critique of Pure Reason, Immanuel Kant: Kant's critique sets the limits of reason: knowledge is restricted to possible experience.
  • Gulliver’s Travels, Jonathan Swift: Swift satirizes human pride and the project-mongering rationalism of the scientists in Laputa.
  • Hamann’s Poetical Works / Schriften, J. G. Hamann: Hamann, Kant's friend and critic, opposes the Enlightenment's autonomous reason with faith, language and revelation.
  • Literary Essays (Vols. II & IV), James Russell Lowell: Lowell's essays on Lessing and on 'Rousseau and the Sentimentalists' assess Enlightenment criticism and sentimental politics.
  • The Age of Ideas, George R. Havens: Havens surveys the French philosophes, especially Voltaire, Montesquieu, Diderot and Rousseau, as campaigners for reason, tolerance and reform before the Revolution.
  • The Social Contract, Jean-Jacques Rousseau: Rousseau argues that legitimate authority comes only from a social contract under the general will.

Ethics and Virtue

  • Essays, Moral, Economical, and Political, Francis Bacon (Boston, 1833): Bacon's essays give practical, worldly-wise counsel on conduct, ambition, friendship and statecraft.
  • Ethics, Benedict de Spinoza: Spinoza argues that freedom and blessedness come from understanding the causes of our emotions, culminating in the intellectual love of God.
  • On the Genealogy of Morals, Friedrich Nietzsche: Nietzsche argues that 'good/evil' morality grew from the ressentiment of the weak against 'good/bad' noble values, and that guilt grew from debt.
  • Oxford Student’s Aristotle, Vol. V: Ethics, Aristotle (Oxford Student’s edition): Aristotle holds that the human good is eudaimonia, activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, with each virtue a mean found by practical wisdom.
  • The Rebel, Albert Camus: Camus argues that rebellion affirms a human limit and solidarity ('I rebel, therefore we exist'), and condemns revolutions that justify murder.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato argues that virtue is the soul's inner harmony (wisdom, courage, moderation and justice) and that justice is worth choosing for its own sake, not just for its rewards.
  • The World as Will and Representation, Vol. I, Arthur Schopenhauer: Schopenhauer founds ethics on compassion, recognizing the same will in all beings, and praises ascetic denial of the will.

Existence and Authenticity

  • Being and Time, Martin Heidegger: Heidegger contrasts the inauthentic 'they-self' with authentic existence grounded in resolute being-toward-death.
  • Concluding Unscientific Postscript, Søren Kierkegaard (tr. Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong): Kierkegaard holds that 'subjectivity is truth': existential truth is passionate inward commitment, not objective knowledge.
  • Repetition and Philosophical Crumbs, Søren Kierkegaard: In Repetition and Philosophical Crumbs, Kierkegaard explores how existence is renewed by repetition and how truth must be received from a teacher (the god) in a moment.
  • The Rebel, Albert Camus: Camus holds that metaphysical and historical revolt must keep a sense of limit and measure.

Faust and the Demonic Pact

  • Doktor Faustus, Thomas Mann: Mann's Leverkühn trades love for creative genius in a deliberate modern Faust.
  • Music, Love, Death and Mann’s Doctor Faustus, Fetzer: Fetzer studies how Mann's novel ties the Faust pact to music, love and death.
  • The Essential Goethe, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe: Goethe's Faust (in the Essential Goethe selections) redeems a striving man who wagers his soul for a moment of fulfillment.

Freedom and Liberty

  • Ethics, Benedict de Spinoza: Spinoza denies free will but holds that freedom is acting from the necessity of one's own nature through adequate understanding.
  • Napoleon the Little, Victor Hugo (London, 1882): Hugo denounces Louis-Napoléon's 1851 coup as the destruction of French liberty by a mediocre usurper.
  • On Experience, Nature, and Freedom, John Dewey: Dewey treats freedom as the growing power of intelligent choice, achieved through experience and social conditions.
  • On the Aesthetic Education of Man, Friedrich Schiller: Schiller holds that freedom is reached through beauty, which reconciles reason and sense.
  • The Brothers Karamazov, Fyodor Dostoevsky: The Grand Inquisitor argues that humanity cannot bear Christ's gift of freedom and prefers bread, miracle and authority.
  • The Philosophy of History, G. W. F. Hegel (tr. J. Sibree): Hegel holds that 'world history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom', from one free (despot) to some (Greeks) to all (Christian-Germanic world).
  • The Social Contract, Jean-Jacques Rousseau: Rousseau holds that people are free under the law when they obey the general will they help make, so that someone may be 'forced to be free'.
  • Walden and Other Writings, Henry David Thoreau: Thoreau holds that the individual conscience is free to refuse unjust laws.

French Revolution and Napoleon

  • Lafayette Letters, Edited by Edward Everett Dale: Lafayette's letters connect the American and French revolutions.
  • Major Works, Vol. I, Joseph de Maistre: Maistre interprets the Revolution as providential punishment and denies the Enlightenment's political premises.
  • Napoleon the Little, Victor Hugo (London, 1882): Hugo's pamphlet attacks Napoleon III's seizure of power as a parody of Napoleon I.
  • The Age of Ideas, George R. Havens: Havens traces the ideas of the philosophes that prepared the Revolution.
  • The Age of Napoleon, Will Durant (with Ariel Durant): Durant narrates Europe from 1789 to 1815, presenting Napoleon as a genius whose ambition both spread and betrayed the Revolution.

God and the Divine

  • A Return to Love, Marianne Williamson: Williamson, teaching A Course in Miracles, holds that God is love and that fear is an illusion to be released.
  • Critique of Pure Reason, Immanuel Kant: Kant argues that God's existence can neither be proved nor disproved by speculative reason; the ontological, cosmological and teleological proofs all fail.
  • Ethics, Benedict de Spinoza: Spinoza identifies God with Nature, a necessary, impersonal substance without purposes.
  • Holy Bible (American Standard Version, with references), American Standard Version: The Bible presents the God of Israel and of Christ as creator, lawgiver and redeemer.
  • Milton and the Sons of God, MacCallum: MacCallum studies Milton's theology of the Son and of angelic and human 'sons of God'.
  • Paradise Lost, John Milton: Milton aims to 'justify the ways of God to men', defending providence and free will through the Fall.
  • Process and Reality, corrected edition, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead conceives God as dipolar: the ground of possibilities and 'the great companion' who preserves the world.
  • Science and the Modern World, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead holds that religion is the vision of something beyond the passing flux and that God is required as a principle of limitation.
  • The Divine Comedy, Dante Alighieri: Dante's ascent ends in the vision of God as the love 'that moves the sun and the other stars'.
  • The Idea of the Holy, Rudolf Otto: Otto holds that the holy is a non-rational 'numinous' experience of mysterium tremendum et fascinans, prior to ethics and concepts.
  • The Metaphysics, Aristotle (tr. Hugh Lawson-Tancred, Penguin Classics): Aristotle argues for an unmoved mover, pure thought thinking itself, as the final cause of motion.

Heroes and Great Men

  • Alexander: The Ends of the Earth, Valerio Massimo Manfredi: Manfredi presents Alexander as a visionary conqueror.
  • Carlyle, Emery Neff: Neff traces how Carlyle developed his hero-worship and his view of history.
  • Frederick the Great (Vols. I–IV), Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle treats Frederick as a hero who saw reality and did work in a sham age, embodying his great-man view of history.
  • George Washington: The Making of an American Symbol, Barry Schwartz: Schwartz argues that Washington's heroic stature was a social construction serving national needs.
  • Living Biographies of Famous Rulers, Henry Thomas and Dana Lee Thomas: Popular lives of rulers presented as exemplary or cautionary.
  • The Hero with a Thousand Faces, Joseph Campbell: Campbell argues that the world's hero myths share a single 'monomyth' of departure, initiation and return.

Justice

  • Oxford Student’s Aristotle, Vol. V: Ethics, Aristotle (Oxford Student’s edition): Aristotle treats justice as the complete virtue in relation to others, distinguishing distributive from corrective justice.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato's Socrates rejects Thrasymachus's view that justice is the advantage of the stronger, defining justice as each part of the city and of the soul doing its own work, and argues that the just life is happier than the unjust.
  • The Social Contract, Jean-Jacques Rousseau: Rousseau holds that justice in political society comes from law made by the general will, replacing natural instinct with duty.

Knowledge and Reason

  • Critique of Pure Reason, Immanuel Kant: Kant argues that synthetic a priori knowledge is possible because space, time and the categories structure experience, but that reason overreaches into antinomies when it goes beyond experience.
  • Essays, Moral, Economical, and Political, Francis Bacon (Boston, 1833): Bacon's essays reflect his worldly, empirical wisdom; 'Of Studies' says reading makes a full man.
  • Gödel, Escher, Bach, Douglas Hofstadter: Hofstadter uses Gödel's incompleteness theorem to show that formal systems rich enough to describe themselves have truths they cannot prove.
  • Hamann’s Poetical Works / Schriften, J. G. Hamann: Hamann argues against Kant that reason is inseparable from language, tradition and faith.
  • Meta-Meditations, Sesonske and Fleming: Sesonske and Fleming's contributors reexamine Descartes' method of doubt, the cogito and his proofs as live problems of certainty and self-knowledge.
  • On Experience, Nature, and Freedom, John Dewey: Dewey treats knowing as active inquiry resolving problematic situations, rather than a spectator copying reality.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Against Descartes, Vico holds that we can truly know only what we have made (verum ipsum factum), so the civil world, made by humans, is more knowable than nature.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato distinguishes knowledge of the unchanging Forms from opinion about the changing sensible world, through the divided line and the allegory of the cave.
  • Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Ludwig Wittgenstein: Wittgenstein holds that what can be said can be said clearly and that whereof one cannot speak one must be silent.

Language and Meaning

  • Hamann’s Poetical Works / Schriften, J. G. Hamann: Hamann holds that language is the first and last organ of reason, and that 'poetry is the mother-tongue of the human race'.
  • Logic, Martin Heidegger: Heidegger argues that logic must be reconceived as the question of the essence of language, which he ties to the historical existence of a people.
  • Shakespeare’s Language, Frank Kermode: Kermode argues that around 1600 Shakespeare's verse grew denser and more difficult, and follows that change play by play.
  • The Gutenberg Galaxy, Marshall McLuhan: McLuhan argues that the shift from oral to print culture reshaped language and thought.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Vico argues that language developed from gestures and mute signs through heroic metaphor to the vernacular speech of the age of men, and uses etymology as evidence for early thought.
  • Thirty-Eight Latin Stories, Anne H. Groton and James M. May: Groton and May provide graded Latin readings for students, adapted from Roman myth and history.
  • Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Ludwig Wittgenstein: Wittgenstein's picture theory holds that propositions picture possible facts, and that logic, ethics and the mystical lie beyond what can be said.
  • Ulysses, James Joyce: Joyce makes language itself the protagonist, shifting style in every chapter of Ulysses.
  • Wheelock’s Latin, Frederic M. Wheelock: Wheelock's grammar teaches Latin through classical-author readings; it is a language course, not an argument.

Literary Criticism

  • Among My Books, James Russell Lowell: Lowell's essays on Dante, Chaucer, Spenser, Wordsworth and others combine erudition with genteel moral judgment.
  • Flaubert, Émile Faguet: Faguet judges Flaubert as a Romantic who disciplined himself into realism through style.
  • From Proust to Camus, André Maurois: Maurois surveys twentieth-century French writers from Proust to Camus.
  • Literary Essays (Vols. II & IV), James Russell Lowell: Lowell's Dante essay treats the Commedia as a universal spiritual journey and is among the most admired nineteenth-century American readings of Dante.
  • Mark Twain’s Wound: A Casebook, Edited by Lewis Leary: Leary collects criticism on whether Twain's genius was wounded by a genteel culture (Van Wyck Brooks vs Bernard DeVoto).
  • Music, Love, Death and Mann’s Doctor Faustus, Fetzer: Fetzer reads Mann's Doctor Faustus through its music, eros and death.
  • My Study Windows, James Russell Lowell: Lowell's essays on Chaucer, Pope, Thoreau and others include a well-known essay that judges Thoreau a self-absorbed and limited writer.
  • Poets on Poetry, Edited by Norman: Poets reflect on their own craft (editor identification not confirmed).
  • Selected Essays of William Hazlitt, William Hazlitt: Hazlitt writes impressionistic, passionate criticism of Shakespeare, the Romantics and the arts.
  • Shakespeare Studies: Macbeth, Charlotte Porter and Helen A. Clarke (American Book Company): A school edition of Macbeth with notes and study apparatus.
  • Shakespeare’s Language, Frank Kermode: Kermode argues that Shakespeare's language becomes more difficult and dense in the mature plays, tracking that change play by play.
  • The Dyer’s Hand, W. H. Auden: Auden argues that poetry is a 'game of knowledge' and that the critic's job is to illuminate, not to rank.
  • The Secular Scripture, Northrop Frye: Frye argues that all romance shares a common structure of descent and ascent.
  • Whatever Is Moving, Howard Moss: Moss writes essays on poets and poetry from his long tenure as The New Yorker's poetry editor.

Love

  • A Return to Love, Marianne Williamson: Williamson holds that love is our true nature and that miracles are shifts from fear to love.
  • Shakespeare’s Sonnets, William Shakespeare: Shakespeare's sonnets treat love as a struggle against time, set against desire and betrayal.
  • The Brothers Karamazov, Fyodor Dostoevsky: Zosima teaches that hell is 'the suffering of being unable to love' and preaches active love.
  • The Divine Comedy, Dante Alighieri: Dante's love for Beatrice leads him toward divine love.

Milton

  • John Milton: Englishman, James Holly Hanford: Hanford argues that Milton's poetry and prose are rooted in English Puritanism, the Civil War and the Commonwealth.
  • Literary Essays (Vols. II & IV), James Russell Lowell: Lowell's Milton essay (Vol. IV) praises the grandeur of Milton's verse while criticizing pedantic editorial scholarship.
  • Milton and the Sons of God, MacCallum: MacCallum studies how Milton's major poems treat the Son of God and the angelic and human 'sons of God', reading theology and poetics together.
  • Paradise Lost, John Milton: Milton's epic aims to 'justify the ways of God to men', presenting Satan's grandeur and fall and Adam and Eve's free choice.
  • The Poetical Works of John Milton, Vol. II (London, 1808), John Milton: An 1808 London edition of Milton's poetical works (contents of this volume not confirmed).

Mysticism

  • A Return to Love, Marianne Williamson: Williamson presents A Course in Miracles as a spiritual path from fear to love.
  • The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, William Blake: Blake's visionary writings hold that the 'doors of perception', once cleansed, would show everything as infinite.
  • The Essential Writings of Christian Mysticism, Associated with Bernard McGinn: McGinn's anthology presents Christian mysticism as the pursuit of direct consciousness of God's presence, through writers from Origen and Pseudo-Dionysius to Eckhart and John of the Cross.
  • Time and Eternity, Walter Terence Stace: Stace holds that the mystical 'eternal moment' is a genuine apprehension of God that intellect can only express in paradox.

Myth and Symbol

  • Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake, Northrop Frye: Frye reads Blake's prophetic books as a unified mythic system.
  • Man and His Symbols, Carl G. Jung: Jung holds that symbols in dreams and myths arise from archetypes in the collective unconscious.
  • Myths of Crete & Pre-Hellenic Europe, Donald A. Mackenzie: Mackenzie retells Cretan and pre-Hellenic myths, linking them to Minoan archaeology and comparative folklore.
  • The Hero with a Thousand Faces, Joseph Campbell: Campbell interprets myths psychologically as variations of a monomyth expressing universal truths about the psyche.
  • The Key to Understand C. G. Jung, Benoit Rousseau: A short guide to Jung's archetypes, the collective unconscious and individuation.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Vico reads myths as 'true narrations' of early peoples, who thought in vivid poetic universals: Jove, for instance, is the first gentiles' image of the thundering sky.
  • The Oxford Dictionary of Classical Myth and Religion, Edited by Simon Price and Emily Kearns: A reference dictionary of Greek and Roman myth, gods, cults and religious practice.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato uses myths of his own, the 'noble lie' of metals and the Myth of Er on judgment and reincarnation, to convey what argument cannot.
  • The Sacred and the Profane, Mircea Eliade: Eliade holds that religious man lives in sacred space and cyclical sacred time renewed by myth and ritual, which modern profane man has lost.
  • The Secular Scripture, Northrop Frye: Frye argues that romance is the 'secular scripture', a structure of stories parallel to the Bible.

Nature

  • African Game Trails, Vol. IV, Theodore Roosevelt: Roosevelt records his 1909 African safari as big-game hunting and natural-history collecting.
  • Creative Evolution, Henri Bergson: Bergson argues that evolution is driven by a creative élan vital rather than mechanism or final design.
  • Essays and Lectures, Ralph Waldo Emerson (Library of America): Emerson's Nature holds that natural facts are symbols of spiritual facts.
  • Process and Reality, corrected edition, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead treats nature as a web of experiential processes rather than inert matter.
  • The Earth Has a Soul, C. G. Jung, edited by Meredith Sabini: Jung argues that modern people suffer from a severed relation to nature and should restore it through soul and instinct.
  • Walden and Other Writings, Henry David Thoreau: Thoreau finds spiritual and moral truth in close, daily observation of nature at Walden.

Phenomenology

  • Being and Time, Martin Heidegger: Heidegger turns Husserl's phenomenology into a hermeneutic analysis of everyday being-in-the-world, care and temporality.
  • Ideas, Edmund Husserl: Husserl introduces the epoché and the transcendental reduction, through which essences of conscious experience can be described.
  • Logos and Life, Anna-Teresa Tymieniecka: Tymieniecka develops a 'phenomenology of life' in which a creative logos of life unfolds through all levels of existence.

Philosophy of History

  • Frederick the Great (Vols. I–IV), Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle sees history as shaped by heroes who perceive and act on the truth of things.
  • Rethinking the Scientific Revolution, Edited by Margaret J. Osler: Osler's contributors show how the 'Scientific Revolution' was itself a historians' construct to be revised.
  • The Decline of the West, Vol. II, Oswald Spengler: Spengler holds that history consists of separate cultures, each with an organic life cycle, rather than linear progress.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Vico argues that every gentile nation passes through an age of gods, an age of heroes and an age of men, and that decline brings a return (ricorso) to barbarism; history follows an 'ideal eternal history.'
  • The Outline of History, H. G. Wells: Wells treats history as a single human story tending toward world unity.
  • The Philosophy of History, G. W. F. Hegel (tr. J. Sibree): Hegel views history as the rational progress of Spirit's freedom, guided by the 'cunning of reason' through passionate individuals.

Poetry and Poetics

  • Contemporary American Poetry, Edited by A. Poulin Jr.: Poulin's anthology presents post-war American poets with their own statements on poetics.
  • George Herbert, Stanley Stewart: Stewart reads Herbert's devotional lyrics as carefully ordered religious art.
  • Great American Prose Poems, Edited by David Lehman: Lehman's anthology argues by example that the prose poem is a vital American form, from Poe and Emerson onward.
  • One Hundred and One Famous Poems, Anthology: A popular anthology of widely memorized English and American poems, valuable as a record of popular taste rather than as argument.
  • Philosophy of Poetry (The Genius of Lucretius), Henri Bergson: Bergson holds that poetry such as Lucretius's can do philosophical work by making ideas felt.
  • Poets on Poetry, Edited by Norman: Practising poets reflect on how poems are made and why they matter (editor identification not confirmed).
  • Shakespeare’s Sonnets, William Shakespeare: Shakespeare's sonnets claim that verse can outlast time and give immortality to the beloved.
  • The Dyer’s Hand, W. H. Auden: Auden holds that a poem is a verbal society ordered by the poet, and reflects on poetry as craft and vocation.
  • The New Pocket Anthology of American Verse, Edited by Oscar Williams: Oscar Williams's pocket anthology spans American verse from colonial times to the mid-twentieth century.
  • The New Science, Giambattista Vico (tr. Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch): Vico's 'poetic wisdom' holds that the first humans were poets who spoke in images; his 'Discovery of the True Homer' treats Homer as the collective voice of the Greek people.
  • The Poetical Works of John Milton, Vol. II (London, 1808), John Milton: An 1808 edition of Milton's poetry, including his shorter poems.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato argues that imitative poetry is at a third remove from truth and feeds the passions, so it is banished from the ideal city except for hymns to gods and praise of good men.
  • Whatever Is Moving, Howard Moss: Moss's essays practice close, appreciative reading of modern poets.

Political Philosophy and the State

  • Essays, Moral, Economical, and Political, Francis Bacon (Boston, 1833): Bacon's essays on empire, sedition and counsel give realist advice to rulers.
  • History of Political Philosophy, Edited by Leo Strauss and Joseph Cropsey: Strauss and Cropsey's contributors survey political philosophy from Thucydides and Plato to Dewey and Heidegger, emphasizing the classical question of the best regime.
  • Major Works, Vol. I, Joseph de Maistre: Maistre holds that constitutions cannot be written by reason and that sovereignty rests on divine providence.
  • Political Essays, James Russell Lowell: Lowell argues that American democracy depends on an educated public conscience, and judges Lincoln and the Union cause by that standard.
  • The Essential Works of Lenin, V. I. Lenin: Lenin's State and Revolution holds that the bourgeois state must be smashed and will wither away under communism.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato builds an ideal city of rulers, auxiliaries and producers ruled by philosopher-kings, and traces its decline through timocracy, oligarchy and democracy to tyranny.
  • The Social Contract, Jean-Jacques Rousseau: Rousseau argues that the sovereign is the people and that law must express the general will.

Prussia and Germany

  • Doktor Faustus, Thomas Mann: Mann's novel allegorizes Germany's path to Nazism through a composer's demonic pact.
  • Frederick the Great (Vols. I–IV), Thomas Carlyle: Carlyle narrates the rise of Prussia under Frederick William I and Frederick II as the building of a disciplined, effective state.

Psychology and the Unconscious

  • Man and His Symbols, Carl G. Jung: Jung holds that the unconscious compensates for one-sided consciousness and guides individuation through dream symbols; the volume adds essays by his collaborators.
  • Mark Twain’s Wound: A Casebook, Edited by Lewis Leary: Leary's casebook debates Van Wyck Brooks's claim that Twain's creative psyche was wounded by a repressive genteel culture.
  • On the Genealogy of Morals, Friedrich Nietzsche: Nietzsche analyzes 'bad conscience' as instinct turned inward once humans were enclosed in society.
  • The Culture of Narcissism: American Life in an Age of Diminishing Expectations, Christopher Lasch: Lasch adapts the psychoanalytic theory of narcissism (Kernberg, Kohut) to diagnose American culture.
  • The Earth Has a Soul, C. G. Jung, edited by Meredith Sabini: Sabini's selection gathers Jung's view that the psyche is rooted in nature and that modern rootlessness is a psychological wound.
  • The Key to Understand C. G. Jung, Benoit Rousseau: An introductory guide to Jung's archetypes, collective unconscious, psychological types and individuation.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato divides the soul into reason, spirit and appetite, and holds that psychological health is reason ruling the other two.

Renaissance and Humanism

  • The Divine Comedy, Dante Alighieri: Dante's Comedy stands at the threshold of the Renaissance, synthesizing medieval theology and classical learning.
  • The Gutenberg Galaxy, Marshall McLuhan: McLuhan argues that print produced the individualist, nationalist and visual mind of the Renaissance.
  • The Scientific Renaissance 1450–1630, Marie Boas Hall: Boas Hall argues that the Renaissance recovery of ancient science both helped and hindered the rise of modern science.
  • The World of Humanism, 1453–1517: The Rise of Modern Europe, Myron P. Gilmore: Gilmore presents 1453–1517 as the period in which humanism, printing and new states transformed Europe.

Revolution

  • Impressions of Lenin, Angelica Balabanoff: Balabanoff, who broke with the Bolsheviks in 1921, argues that their indifference to means corrupted the revolution's socialist ends.
  • John Adams, Vol. I (1735–1784), Page Smith: Smith shows Adams as a leading advocate of independence in the Continental Congress, insisting that the Revolution must be grounded in law and balanced government.
  • Major Works, Vol. I, Joseph de Maistre: Maistre argues that the Revolution was a providential chastisement that its own actors did not control.
  • Stalin, Leon Trotsky: Trotsky presents Stalin's rise as the bureaucracy's Thermidor, a betrayal of the October Revolution.
  • The Age of Napoleon, Will Durant (with Ariel Durant): Durant traces how the Revolution's energies were channelled and ultimately subordinated by Napoleon's empire.
  • The Essential Works of Lenin, V. I. Lenin: Lenin argues that a disciplined vanguard party is needed to carry out proletarian revolution.
  • The Rebel, Albert Camus: Camus argues that revolutions which abandon the limit set by rebellion end in state terror.

Satire

  • Babbitt, Sinclair Lewis: Lewis satirizes the boosterism and conformity of American business culture.
  • Gulliver’s Travels, Jonathan Swift: Swift satirizes human pride and folly, ending with the rational Houyhnhnms against the bestial Yahoos.
  • Napoleon the Little, Victor Hugo (London, 1882): Hugo's invective mocks Napoleon III as a petty imitator of his uncle.

Science and Religion

  • Rethinking the Scientific Revolution, Edited by Margaret J. Osler: Essays reassess religion's positive role in seventeenth-century natural philosophy.
  • Science and the Modern World, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead holds that modern science grew from medieval faith in an ordered, rational nature, and that science and religion must keep revising each other.
  • Time and Eternity, Walter Terence Stace: Stace holds that religious truth (the eternal) and naturalistic science (the temporal order) occupy different orders and do not conflict.

Science and the Scientific Revolution

  • Greek Science in Antiquity, Marshall Clagett: Clagett traces Greek science and argues that Greek mathematics and natural philosophy were transmitted through late antiquity to the medieval West.
  • Rethinking the Scientific Revolution, Edited by Margaret J. Osler: Osler's contributors question 'the Scientific Revolution' as a single sharp break, stressing continuities and the role of religion, alchemy and context.
  • Science and the Modern World, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead argues that modern science was built on 'scientific materialism' and the 'fallacy of misplaced concreteness', and calls for an organic philosophy instead.
  • The Origins of Modern Science, Herbert Butterfield: Butterfield argues that the Scientific Revolution 'outshines everything since the rise of Christianity', with the key change being a new 'thinking cap' rather than new data.
  • The Scientific Renaissance 1450–1630, Marie Boas Hall: Boas Hall describes 1450–1630 as a renaissance in which humanist recovery of ancient texts, craft knowledge and observation prepared the new science.
  • The World of Humanism, 1453–1517: The Rise of Modern Europe, Myron P. Gilmore: Gilmore relates humanism and the printing press to the changing intellectual world of the fifteenth century.

Self-Reliance and Individualism

  • Essays and Lectures, Ralph Waldo Emerson (Library of America): Emerson ('Self-Reliance', 'The American Scholar') urges trust in one's own intuition over conformity, tradition and institutions: 'Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist.'
  • Representative Man: Ralph Waldo Emerson in His Time, Joel Porte: Porte follows how Emerson made himself the spokesman for American self-culture.
  • Ride the Tiger, Julius Evola: Evola's ideal is the inwardly sovereign individual who stays detached from the collapsing modern world.
  • Walden and Other Writings, Henry David Thoreau: Thoreau's Walden argues for simplifying life to live deliberately, and 'Civil Disobedience' holds that conscience outranks law.

Shakespeare

  • Selected Essays of William Hazlitt, William Hazlitt: Hazlitt's essays include character-centred, passionate criticism of Shakespeare.
  • Shakespeare Studies: Macbeth, Charlotte Porter and Helen A. Clarke (American Book Company): A nineteenth-century American classroom study of Macbeth.
  • Shakespeare’s Language, Frank Kermode: Kermode argues that Shakespeare's language grew denser and more difficult after about 1600, and follows that change play by play.
  • Shakespeare’s Sonnets, William Shakespeare: The sonnets explore love, time and betrayal through the young man and the dark lady.
  • The Tragedy of King Lear, William Shakespeare (intro & notes by Mary Barron): A school edition of King Lear with introduction and notes.

Sin, Evil and the Fall

  • Doktor Faustus, Thomas Mann: Mann links the composer's pact with the devil to Germany's fall into Nazism.
  • On the Genealogy of Morals, Friedrich Nietzsche: Nietzsche traces 'evil' to the ressentiment of slave morality against the strong.
  • Paradise Lost, John Milton: Milton portrays Satan's rebellion and humanity's Fall, insisting that God made humans 'sufficient to have stood, though free to fall'.
  • Shakespeare Studies: Macbeth, Charlotte Porter and Helen A. Clarke (American Book Company): Macbeth shows ambition leading to murder and moral ruin.
  • The Brothers Karamazov, Fyodor Dostoevsky: Ivan's rebellion rejects a harmony bought with the suffering of innocent children.
  • The Divine Comedy, Dante Alighieri: Dante's Inferno orders sins by their severity, with treachery deepest.
  • Three Complete Novels, Nathaniel Hawthorne: Hawthorne's novels (which include The Scarlet Letter) explore hidden sin, guilt and Puritan judgment.

Technology and Media

  • Infinite Jest, David Foster Wallace: Wallace imagines a film so entertaining it kills viewers.
  • Limits to Medicine, Ivan Illich: Illich argues that modern medicine becomes counterproductive past a threshold, producing iatrogenic harm.
  • Simulacra and Simulation, Jean Baudrillard: Baudrillard argues that simulations have replaced the real, producing a 'hyperreal' without original.
  • The Gutenberg Galaxy, Marshall McLuhan: McLuhan argues that print created 'typographic man', fragmenting perception and favoring the eye.
  • The Technological Society, Jacques Ellul: Ellul argues that 'technique', the pursuit of absolute efficiency, has become autonomous and governs modern society.

The Absolute and Spirit

  • Essays and Lectures, Ralph Waldo Emerson (Library of America): Emerson posits an Over-Soul, a universal mind in which every person participates, so that intuition gives direct access to the divine source behind nature.
  • Ethics, Benedict de Spinoza: Spinoza identifies God with the one infinite substance (Deus sive Natura), of which minds and bodies are modes; nothing exists or is conceived outside it.
  • Process and Reality, corrected edition, Alfred North Whitehead: Whitehead rejects a static Absolute: God has a primordial nature (the envisagement of all possibilities) and a consequent nature that receives and preserves the world's becoming.
  • The Philosophy of History, G. W. F. Hegel (tr. J. Sibree): Hegel holds that world history is the rational self-unfolding of Spirit (Geist) toward consciousness of its own freedom, so that 'the real is rational' at the scale of history.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato's Form of the Good, compared to the sun, is the source of both the being and the intelligibility of all that is known.
  • Time and Eternity, Walter Terence Stace: Stace argues that the eternal, as known in mystical experience, is a different order from the temporal and cannot be reached or refuted by naturalistic reasoning about the time-order.

The Novel and Modern Fiction

  • Flaubert, Émile Faguet: Faguet presents Flaubert as the novelist who made style and impersonality the discipline of realism.
  • From Proust to Camus, André Maurois: Maurois surveys the French novel and essay from Proust's memory to Camus's absurd.
  • Great Expectations, Charles Dickens: Dickens's novel shows Pip learning that gentility and expectations matter less than loyalty and gratitude.
  • Infinite Jest, David Foster Wallace: Wallace's novel weaves a tennis academy and a recovery house into a study of addiction and entertainment.
  • Light in August, William Faulkner: Faulkner's novel braids the stories of Lena Grove, Joe Christmas and Hightower.
  • Mason & Dixon, Thomas Pynchon: Pynchon's novel imitates eighteenth-century prose to show the Enlightenment imposing order on the American wilderness.
  • The Name of the Rose, Umberto Eco: Eco's novel uses a medieval murder mystery to explore signs, interpretation and the danger of laughter to authority.
  • Three Complete Novels, Nathaniel Hawthorne: Hawthorne's novels treat New England's Puritan past as a source of moral romance.
  • Ulysses, James Joyce: Joyce's Ulysses follows one Dublin day as a modern Odyssey, using stream of consciousness and shifting styles.

The Sacred and the Holy

  • The Idea of the Holy, Rudolf Otto: Otto describes the holy as the 'wholly other', evoking awe, dread and fascination.
  • The Oxford Dictionary of Classical Myth and Religion, Edited by Simon Price and Emily Kearns: This dictionary covers Greek and Roman cults, sanctuaries and religious practice.
  • The Sacred and the Profane, Mircea Eliade: Eliade argues that sacred space and time are made by hierophanies, manifestations of the sacred.

Time and Eternity

  • Being and Time, Martin Heidegger: Heidegger argues that temporality is the meaning of Dasein's being.
  • Creative Evolution, Henri Bergson: Bergson holds that real time is duration, a creative flow that intellect falsifies by spatializing it.
  • Repetition and Philosophical Crumbs, Søren Kierkegaard: Kierkegaard treats repetition as the forward recovery of a life, and the 'moment' as where the eternal enters time.
  • Shakespeare’s Sonnets, William Shakespeare: Shakespeare's sonnets set love and verse against devouring time.
  • The Sacred and the Profane, Mircea Eliade: Eliade argues that religious man periodically returns to the mythical time of origins.
  • Time and Eternity, Walter Terence Stace: Stace holds that the eternal and temporal orders intersect in mystical experience without competing.

Tragedy

  • Doktor Faustus, Thomas Mann: Mann's novel is a modern tragedy of genius, a pact with the devil and national catastrophe.
  • Euripides III, Euripides (ed. David Grene and Richmond Lattimore): Euripides' tragedies question the justice of the gods and expose the human cost of war and passion.
  • Shakespeare Studies: Macbeth, Charlotte Porter and Helen A. Clarke (American Book Company): A classroom study of Macbeth's ambition, guilt and moral disintegration.
  • The Birth of Tragedy and The Case of Wagner, Friedrich Nietzsche: Nietzsche holds that tragedy arose from the Dionysian chorus and was killed by Euripides' and Socrates' rational optimism.
  • The Dramatic Works of William Shakespeare, Vol. II (Hartford, 1832), William Shakespeare: A volume of an 1832 American edition of Shakespeare's plays (exact plays in this volume not confirmed).
  • The Tragedy of King Lear, William Shakespeare (intro & notes by Mary Barron): Lear dramatizes the collapse of kingship and family through filial ingratitude, madness and suffering that may be meaningless.

Transcendentalism and Romanticism

  • Essays and Lectures, Ralph Waldo Emerson (Library of America): Emerson's Nature and essays hold that nature symbolizes spirit and that each soul can perceive the divine directly.
  • Fearful Symmetry: A Study of William Blake, Northrop Frye: Frye argues that Blake has a coherent visionary system in which imagination is reality and his prophecies form a single myth.
  • Poems of Byron, Vol. II, Lord Byron: Byron's poems embody the Romantic rebel, defiant and ironic, set against convention.
  • The American Romantics, 1800–1860 (American Literature Survey), American Literature Survey (revised edition): A survey anthology of American Romantic writers from 1800 to 1860.
  • The Complete Poetry and Prose of William Blake, William Blake: Blake holds that imagination is the divine in man and attacks Newtonian reason and Lockean empiricism.
  • The Essential Goethe, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe: Goethe's writings, from Werther to Faust, move from Sturm und Drang toward a classical balance of striving and limit.
  • The Romantic Rebellion, Kenneth Clark: Clark presents Romanticism in art as a revolt of feeling, imagination and the sublime against classical order.
  • Walden and Other Writings, Henry David Thoreau: Thoreau grounds transcendentalism in daily practice, reading Walden Pond as a living scripture.

Travel

  • African Game Trails, Vol. IV, Theodore Roosevelt: Roosevelt's narrative of his 1909–10 safari through East Africa.
  • Fireside Travels, James Russell Lowell: Lowell's sketches of Cambridge, the Mediterranean and Italy mix travel impressions with reflection.
  • Gulliver’s Travels, Jonathan Swift: Swift's imaginary voyages use travel-writing form for satire.

Utopia and the Ideal Society

  • Hamann’s Poetical Works / Schriften, J. G. Hamann: Swift's Houyhnhnms parody the rationally ordered utopia, leaving Gulliver unable to live among humans.
  • The Republic, Plato (tr. Desmond Lee, Penguin Classics): Plato's city in words, with common property and family among the guardians and rule by philosophers, is the founding Western blueprint of an ideal society.
